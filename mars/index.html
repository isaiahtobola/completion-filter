The Energy

The record — batteries, reactors, and one rescue rule

The night kept coming back to one question: what keeps a machine alive on Mars? The record has two answers — and Isaiah found both of them inside a single message.

From the Bench — The Energy

Two ways to stay alive out there, by the numbers.

Plutonium-238The battery — plutonium dioxide, hot from its own natural decay, turned straight into electricity. No chain reaction, nothing to throttle; it simply lasts for decades
The MMRTGThe box that carries it — 4.8 kilos of plutonium dioxide making about 110 watts at a mission’s start. One rides in Curiosity; one rides in Percy
SolarFree and endless — until the sky itself turns. The planet-wide dust storm of 2018 starved Opportunity’s panels, and dust gathered on her own panels is the leading suspect in Zhurong’s silence
Uranium-235The reactor — true fission, a chain reaction, far more power. Submarines and carriers run on it down here
SNAP-10A1965 — the only fission reactor the United States has ever flown: 500 watts for 43 days, still in orbit today
KilopowerNASA’s Mars-class reactor — a cast uranium-235 core, ground-tested through the winter of 2017–18, built for the Moon and Mars, up to 10 kilowatts a unit
The CounterweightHonesty, kept on the record: freeing Mars’s frozen CO2 is the classic warming sketch, but a 2018 NASA-backed inventory (Jakosky & Edwards) counted the accessible CO2 and found there isn’t enough of it to do that job with present-day technology. The dream survives the audit; the easy version of it doesn’t

And the night added one rule of its own — Isaiah’s. If any of them ever run out, like the rovers did, we send backup robots carrying energetic nuclear cargo, and we keep sending them until every light is back on.

Nobody gets left dark.
